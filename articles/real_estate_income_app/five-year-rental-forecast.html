Will Your Rental Still Pay Off in Five Years? How to Build a Simple Forecast

By Constance Beebe, former hotel general manager with more than 20 years in hospitality

Most rental owners know how last month went. Fewer can tell you how the next five years will go. That gap matters. A cabin that looks fine this year can slip into the red once insurance goes up, the roof needs work, and a slow season shows up at the same time.

In hotels, we never planned only for the next month. Every budget I worked on as a general manager looked ahead, because the costs that hurt you are the ones you see coming too late. A rental is a small hotel with one room. It deserves the same kind of look ahead.

Here is how to build a simple five-year forecast from the records you already have.

Why look five years ahead?

One year hides slow changes. A 4% rise in cleaning fees or a 6% jump in insurance does not feel like much in a single year. Stack those rises on top of each other for five years and they can take a real bite out of what the property earns.

Five years is also short enough to stay honest. You can make reasonable guesses about rent, bookings and costs over five years. Past that, you are mostly guessing.

Step 1: Start with last year's real numbers

A forecast is only as good as the year it starts from. Pull together the last full year of income and expenses for the property. If a property manager or resort rental program handles bookings, your monthly owner statements are the place to start. Record the gross rental income and each fee separately, not just the payout.

Then group your expenses into clear categories: management, cleaning, repairs, utilities, insurance, property tax, supplies, and so on. If your records are scattered, our guide to rental property expense categories can help you sort them.

Step 2: Set your assumptions

Assumptions are the guesses your forecast runs on. Keep them few and write them down so you can check them next year. You need four:

  • Rent. What you expect the property to bring in at full booking, based on your current rates.
  • Vacancy. The share of time the property sits empty. Use your own booking history, not an average you read online.
  • Rent growth. How much you expect your rates to rise each year. Be modest here.
  • Expense growth. How much you expect costs to rise each year. Be less modest here. Costs rarely surprise you on the low side.

Step 3: Count the loan separately

If the property has a mortgage, keep the loan payment out of your operating expenses and look at it on its own line. This shows you two different things. The first is whether the property earns money on its own, which is called net operating income. The second is whether that money covers the loan.

Lenders call that second check debt coverage. Divide the year's net operating income by the year's loan payments. Above 1 means the property pays its own loan with some left over. Below 1 means you are covering the gap yourself.

A month-by-month loan schedule is worth having too. It shows how much of each payment goes to interest and how much pays down the balance. If you are thinking about paying extra principal, the schedule shows how much interest that saves and how much sooner the loan ends.

Step 4: Run a slow year

Your first forecast is the year you expect. Now build a second one with a harder year in it. Raise vacancy, hold rents flat for a year, and add a large repair. Then look at what happens to your cash.

This is the step most owners skip, and it is the one that helps most. If one slow season would push you into covering the mortgage from your own savings, you want to know that now, while you can still build a cushion.

Step 5: Set money aside for taxes

A rental that makes money usually creates a tax bill, and that bill can catch owners off guard in April. Setting aside an estimated tax reserve through the year keeps the surprise smaller.

Depreciation on the building and its improvements can also change what you owe. How your rental is taxed depends on your own situation, so treat any reserve as a planning number and confirm the details with a qualified tax professional.

What should I look for in my forecast?

Read your five years from left to right and ask three questions:

  • Does cash flow hold steady, grow, or shrink over the five years?
  • Does debt coverage stay above 1 in your slow-year version?
  • Is there a year where a planned cost, like a roof or new furniture, needs cash you do not have set aside?

The answers will not tell you whether to keep the property. That is your decision. They will tell you what to watch, what to save for, and what to ask your property manager or tax professional about.

Questions owners ask

How far ahead should a rental property forecast go?

Five years is a practical length. It is long enough to show how rising costs and loan payments add up, and short enough that your assumptions stay close to what you actually know.

What vacancy rate should I use for a vacation rental?

Use your own history first. Look at the nights your property sat empty over the last year or two, then run a second version with a slower year so you know what a bad season does to your cash.

What is debt coverage on a rental property?

Debt coverage compares the money your property earns after operating expenses with what you owe on the loan each year. A number above 1 means the property covers its loan payments with some left over. Below 1 means you are adding money from your own pocket.

Is a rental forecast the same as a tax estimate?

No. A forecast is a planning tool for your cash. Setting money aside for taxes is wise, but how your rental is taxed depends on your situation, so confirm the details with a qualified tax professional.
