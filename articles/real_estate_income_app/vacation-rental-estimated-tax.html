Nobody Takes Tax Out of Your Cabin's Owner Payout, and the IRS Expects It Four Times a Year

By Constance Beebe, former hotel general manager with more than 20 years in hospitality

Your October owner statement from the Pigeon Forge rental program arrived this week. The payout is already in your checking account in Ohio, and nothing was taken out for taxes. The IRS puts rents on its list of income that is not subject to withholding, and its last 2026 estimated tax deadline is January 15, 2027. If the cabin had a good summer, that date is two months away.

This guide is for the owner who lives out of state, lets a property manager or resort rental program run a Gatlinburg or Pigeon Forge cabin, and wants to know how much of each payout is really theirs to spend. It is general information, not tax advice, so take your numbers to a tax professional before you act on them.

Does a property manager take taxes out of my owner payout?

Usually not, and you should plan as if yours does not. The IRS instructions for Form 1040-ES name rents, along with self-employment earnings, interest and dividends, as income that is not subject to withholding. That means the full payout lands in your account, and the tax on any profit is yours to cover.

Look at the bottom of your statement. You will see management commission, cleaning charges, card fees and repairs taken out, but you will rarely see a line for federal income tax. If you do not see one, there is no tax reserve unless you make one.

Do I have to make estimated tax payments on vacation rental income?

You generally must pay estimated tax if you expect to owe at least $1,000 for 2026 after your withholding and refundable credits, and your withholding will fall short of a safe amount. The IRS defines that safe amount as the smaller of 90% of the tax on your 2026 return or 100% of the tax on your 2025 return. If your 2025 adjusted gross income was more than $150,000 ($75,000 if married filing separately), use 110% of last year's tax instead of 100%.

In plain terms, a cabin that loses money on paper may not push you over that line, and a cabin that clears a real profit can. Withholding from a paycheck or pension counts toward the test, so some owners cover the cabin through their other withholding. Which one fits you is a question for your tax professional, and it is easier to answer when your rental numbers are already up to date.

When are estimated tax payments due for 2026?

The four 2026 due dates are April 15, 2026, June 15, 2026, September 15, 2026 and January 15, 2027. You can skip the January payment if you file your 2026 return by February 1, 2027 and pay the full balance with it. The IRS takes payments through IRS Direct Pay from a checking or savings account at no cost, or through your IRS online account.

Your home state may tax this income too, and it will have its own rules and dates. Ask your tax professional to include it when they figure your federal amount.

Is the tax figured on the payout or on the cabin's profit?

On the profit, which is rarely the same number as the payout. The IRS lets you subtract rental expenses such as mortgage interest, real estate taxes, maintenance, utilities, insurance and depreciation from rental income, and you report both on Schedule E.

Some of those costs are already taken out on your owner statement. Others never appear there, because you pay them yourself from home: the mortgage, the county property tax bill, the insurance premium, the HOA dues, the new hot tub cover you bought online. A payout of $3,000 can be far more profit than you think if the statement covers everything, or far less once your own bills are counted. Our guide to reading your property manager's owner statement shows which lines to record, and these ten expense categories catch the costs that never reach the statement.

Do my own stays at the cabin change the tax?

They can. The IRS treats a rental as a residence if your personal use is more than the greater of 14 days or 10% of the days it is rented to others at a fair rental price. When you use it both ways, you generally divide your expenses between rental and personal use by the number of days for each, and you cannot deduct rental expenses beyond your gross rental income, though some of the excess may carry forward.

That Thanksgiving week and the long weekend in October count. Ask your rental program to show owner stays and blocked dates clearly on every statement, and keep your own tally so the day count is not a guess at tax time.

How much of each payout should I set aside for taxes?

Set aside your expected profit times your own tax rates, not a flat share of the payout. Each month, take the payout, subtract the costs you paid yourself, and multiply what is left by your federal marginal rate plus your home state's rate. Move that amount into a separate savings account the day the payout lands, so it is never part of the money you think you have.

Check the running total before each due date. If the year is going better than expected, the reserve grows with it. If a slow winter or a big repair cuts the profit, you see that before January, not after. Your tax professional can tell you which rates to use and whether depreciation changes the picture for you.

Questions owners ask

Do I pay tax on the whole owner payout from my rental program?

Not usually. The IRS lets you subtract rental expenses such as mortgage interest, real estate taxes, maintenance, utilities, insurance and depreciation from rental income. Some of those are already taken out on your statement and some you pay yourself, so your taxable profit is rarely the same as the payout.

Can the tax withheld from my paycheck cover my rental income?

It can count. The IRS estimated tax tests are figured after subtracting your withholding, so withholding from wages or a pension counts toward what you owe. Whether it is enough depends on your whole return, so check it with your tax professional.

What if I rented my cabin for fewer than 15 days this year?

The IRS has a special rule: if you use a dwelling unit as a residence and rent it for fewer than 15 days, you report none of the rental income and deduct none of the expenses as rental expenses.
